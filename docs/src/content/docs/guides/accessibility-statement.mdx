This page says what Voidable aims for in accessibility, what the project checks, what it does not, and where it is known to fall short. It is a statement of effort and evidence. **It is not a claim that Voidable, or a page built with it, conforms to WCAG or any other standard.** Conformance belongs to a finished page, and only an audit of that page can establish it. The [accessibility guide](/guides/accessibility/) covers what the components do for you and what you still have to supply.

## What we aim for

The aim is [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) level AA for the components themselves: keyboard operation, names, roles and states a screen reader can use, focus that is visible and managed, text and control contrast, and respect for the reader's colour scheme, motion and forced-colours settings. "Aim" is the right word. Several of the places where the library falls short today are listed under [Known gaps](#known-gaps), and a component can pass every automated check below and still fail a real user.

## What is tested, and how

Everything in this section runs in the repository, on every pull request unless noted.

- **axe-core.** The browser suite runs axe-core on a page with every component, in the dark and light theme, and on each open overlay, with the rule tags `wcag2a`, `wcag2aa`, `wcag21a`, `wcag21aa`, `wcag22aa` and `best-practice`. It runs in **Chromium only**, not in Firefox or WebKit. Known violations are recorded with a reason in `tests/browser/axe-baseline.json`; a new violation, or a fixed one still listed, fails the suite.
- **Keyboard operation.** Specs drive the composite widgets (tabs, accordion, segmented control, slider, select, menu, calendar, date picker, combobox and multiselect, and the overlays) with real key presses in Chromium, Firefox and WebKit, against the key tables of the WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices: one tab stop, arrow, Home and End movement, Enter and Space, Escape, and where focus goes when an overlay opens and closes. One spec walks the whole kitchen-sink page with Tab to check there is no keyboard trap.
- **Accessibility tree.** Specs assert the role, accessible name, description and states the browser exposes, through Playwright's role queries and ARIA snapshots, in Chromium, Firefox and WebKit. The screen-reader scenarios below are first written as the same assertions against the accessibility tree, from one set of definitions, so a failure with a real screen reader can be told apart from a failure of the page.
- **Chart data tables.** The charts (`void-bar-chart`, `void-line-chart`, `void-donut-chart`, `void-gauge`) are drawings, so each carries a visually hidden data table, named by the chart's `aria-label`, with the drawing hidden from assistive technology. Browser specs assert the table in the accessibility tree (caption, headers, one row per datum, numbers in the language of the page), that the drawing is hidden, that `show-table` shows it, and that it holds in right-to-left; a scripted screen-reader scenario (`chart-table`) moves through the table cell by cell. What a drawn chart means (a trend, an outlier) is not in the table: state the point in the text beside it.
- **Words and locale.** A browser spec renders a page in French and in Arabic (right-to-left) and asserts, in Chromium, Firefox and WebKit, the accessible names and visible strings of a dozen components, the month and weekday names and first day of the week of the calendar, and that a change of `lang` redraws them; a server spec shows that a patch changing `lang` on the root does not reset a component's state. Unit tests cover the fallback from region to language to English, plural forms and that a name or placeholder you write wins. The shipped languages are not reviewed by native speakers: see [Translation](/guides/translation/).
- **Contrast.** `lint:contrast` evaluates the derived theme colours for the default settings, every theme and skin, and the extremes of each colour setting, in dark and light, on the page, on media and on glass. Text must reach 4.5:1 and control borders and focus rings 3:1. A browser spec (`contrast.spec.ts`, Chromium) measures every piece of text on pages showing each colour and variant of badges, tags, alerts, banners, toasts, buttons (outline, filled, ghost), tabs, stats and fields, also inside cards, panels, lists, tables and notices, from the computed styles of rendered components, in dark and light, with no skin and with Loft and Slab, under every shipped theme and under branded accents of each hue family at the softest contrast. Another spec checks the visible boundary of controls (WCAG 1.4.11).
- **Focus visibility.** Another browser spec presses Tab through every keyboard stop of the kitchen-sink page and checks that each one shows a focus indicator that differs from its unfocused state by 3:1.
- **Forced colours and reduced motion.** A spec turns `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` on in every engine and checks that nothing animates forever (a spinner excepted) and that transitions are effectively instant. A second spec turns `forced-colors: active` on and checks that component states stay distinguishable; it runs in **Chromium only**, because that is the only engine Playwright can emulate forced colours in.
- **Target size.** The theme gives every control a hit area of at least 44 by 44 CSS pixels under a coarse pointer (WCAG 2.5.5, the enhanced criterion; 2.5.8 asks for 24). A browser spec emulates a coarse pointer in Chromium, Firefox and WebKit (it first checks that `(pointer: coarse)` matches), clicks at a point just outside the drawn box of each covered control and asserts the control activates, clicks between neighbours in each dense group and asserts the nearer one gets it, and asserts that with a fine pointer no hit-area box is generated. The segmented control, the number input's steppers and the calendar's days are not covered (see [Touch targets](/getting-started/theming/#touch-targets)), and the spec clicks at exact points: it does not model the way a browser adjusts a touch tap to a nearby control.
- **Development warnings and `voidable check`.** These report a missing accessible name, an invalid attribute or a part in the wrong place while you write the markup. They help a page author; they are not an audit.
- **Scripted screen readers.** VoiceOver on macOS and NVDA on Windows are driven by scripts through [Guidepup](https://www.guidepup.dev), and what they actually speak is asserted. See the next section.

### Scripted screen-reader runs

A GitHub Actions workflow (`screen-readers.yml`) starts a real VoiceOver on a macOS runner and a real NVDA on a Windows runner, opens small pages in a real browser, moves through them with the screen reader's own navigation commands (next item, next heading, next form control, next landmark, activate) and checks what was spoken. The scenarios cover buttons, a link button and an icon-only button; a field with a required and an invalid input and its error; a checkbox, a radio group, a switch, a slider and a number input; a dialog; a dropdown menu; tabs; an accordion and a collapsible; a toast and an alert; a tooltip; a table; breadcrumbs and pagination; and a select, a combobox and a multiselect.

The assertions are deliberately loose. They look for the name, a word for the role and a word for each state in what was said, never a whole sentence, because VoiceOver and NVDA, and their versions, word things differently. They run with the screen reader and the system in English only.

These runs are not part of the release gate. They run on pull requests that touch the components or the theme, once a week, and on demand, and they may fail for reasons that are not the library's (a runner image change, a screen reader update). A failure is investigated, not ignored, but it does not stop a release.

## What is not tested

Be clear about what the checks above are not.

- **No audit by screen-reader users.** No person who relies on a screen reader has tested Voidable, and no accessibility consultancy has audited it. A script that presses keys and reads a log finds regressions and gross errors. It does not tell whether a flow is understandable or pleasant to use.
- **No JAWS, no TalkBack, no VoiceOver on iOS.** Only VoiceOver on macOS and NVDA on Windows are scripted, each with one browser. Narrator, Orca, Dragon, switch access, magnifiers and braille displays are not tested. The [screen reader results](/guides/screen-reader-results/#what-has-not-been-tested) page lists this and has a checklist and a [report form](https://github.com/VoidableUI/Voidable/issues/new?template=assistive-technology-report.yml) for a mobile pass.
- **No screen reader in other languages.** The French and Arabic messages have been reviewed for grammar and usual interface wording, but no screen reader is run in a language other than English.
- **No cognitive, language or usability evaluation.** Reading level, consistent behaviour, error recovery, time limits and similar concerns are not assessed.
- **Not every combination.** One browser per screen reader, one version of each runner image, desktop only. Zoom and reflow at 400%, and text spacing overrides are not tested by a dedicated check, and touch is tested by emulation, not on a device.
- **Not your page.** The components give you the pieces. Headings, landmarks, names for icon-only controls, focus after your own actions and the meaning of your content are yours; the [accessibility guide](/guides/accessibility/#what-you-still-have-to-provide) lists them.

## Known gaps

These are real, current findings, not a complete audit.

**From the project's own checks.** The `tests/browser/axe-baseline.json` file records the violations axe-core finds today, each with its reason:

- `void-nav-menu` holds plain links inside a `menubar`, which axe reports as missing required children.
- The `void-color-picker` host is a focusable widget around native controls.
- The viewport of `void-scroll-area` cannot be scrolled by keyboard unless its content is focusable.
- Several targets are smaller than 24 by 24 CSS pixels in their drawn box: sidebar and breadcrumb links, carousel indicators, the compact colour input. Under a coarse pointer the breadcrumb links and carousel indicators now have a larger hit area (see Target size above); with a mouse they are unchanged.
- Under the Slab skin a selected list item is inverted, and controls and chips written inside it (badges, tags, outline and ghost buttons) keep the colours of the page and fall below 4.5:1 there. Plain text in the item is fine.

**From real screen readers.** The first scripted runs of VoiceOver (macOS, Safari engine) and NVDA (Windows, Chromium) found these. The accessibility tree is correct in each case; what the reader says is not. They are being worked on, and fixes made so far are attempts that have not yet been confirmed by a real run (issue 75):

- Number input (VoiceOver): the value was spoken as a percentage of the range. Fixed with `aria-valuetext`; confirmed on VoiceOver.
- Toast: neither reader announced a toast. Fixed: the container holds hidden status and alert announcers that speak each toast, and the toast itself is a named group. Confirmed on both VoiceOver and NVDA.
- Dialog description (VoiceOver): the name is spoken on entry and the description is not. This looks like VoiceOver behaviour rather than a component defect: the description is correct in the accessibility tree and NVDA speaks it once. An `aria-description` copy made no difference and was removed.
- Radio (NVDA): the checked radio is spoken as "not checked", without its position in the group. Cause not found.
- Accordion (NVDA): a collapsed trigger is spoken as "expanded". Cause not found.
- Multiselect (VoiceOver): nothing is said about the selection after Enter. Cause not found.
- Dropdown menu (VoiceOver): the first item is not spoken when the menu opens. Cause not found.

## Reporting a problem

If a component does not work with your assistive technology, or you find something on this site that you cannot use, please [open an issue](https://github.com/VoidableUI/Voidable/issues/new) in the repository. It helps to include:

- the component and what you did,
- what you expected to hear or do and what happened,
- your screen reader and its version, browser and operating system, and
- a short piece of markup that shows it, if you can.

Reports from people who use assistive technology every day are the most valuable thing this project is missing, and are welcome. There is a [form for a pass with any assistive technology](https://github.com/VoidableUI/Voidable/issues/new?template=assistive-technology-report.yml); [Browser support](/guides/browser-support/) says which browsers are supported.
